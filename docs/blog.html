From stationary scattering
to a moving quantum wave packet

A one-dimensional electron, a barrier, and two different questions: what is the transmission at one energy, and what happens when a localized quantum state arrives?

1. The time-dependent experiment

The default experiment prepares a normalized Gaussian wavefunction to the left of the barrier. The complex wavefunction then evolves according to the time-dependent Schrödinger equation. The envelope moves, spreads and splits through the numerical dynamics; the animation is a replay of calculated states.

iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = [−C ∂²/∂x² + V(x)]ψ
C = ℏ²/(2mₑ) = 0.0380998212 eV nm²
ℏ = 0.6582119569 eV fs
ψ(x,0) ∝ exp[−(x−x₀)²/(4σₓ²)] exp[ik₀(x−x₀)]

The density has standard deviation σₓ. Its central wave number is k₀ = √(E₀/C), and its momentum width is ℏ/(2σₓ). E₀ labels the central kinetic energy; the mean energy is E₀ + C/(4σₓ²). A wider packet therefore resolves energies more narrowly, which matters near double-barrier resonances.

2. A unitary numerical time step

A centered spatial difference produces a real symmetric tridiagonal Hamiltonian. Crank–Nicolson advances its complex state with

(I + iΔtH/2ℏ) ψⁿ⁺¹ = (I − iΔtH/2ℏ) ψⁿ.

The complex tridiagonal system is solved with precomputed Thomas/LU factors. The default grid is Δx = 0.020 nm, with 0.010 nm available for refinement; Δt = 0.008 fs. Both discretizations are second order. For this finite Hermitian Hamiltonian the step preserves probability up to floating-point roundoff. Conservation is an internal check; agreement with continuum physics still requires convergence and independent comparisons.

A browser worker evolves all spatial samples in double precision. Up to 481 time slices are stored for pause, slow motion and seeking. Plotting samples are downsampled only after the full-grid probabilities are calculated. Playback speed never changes the numerical time step.

3. Boundaries and what the percentages mean

The computation box is −80 to +80 nm, with zero wavefunction on outside ghost points. These distant walls are reflecting, not transparent. Each stored time step checks probability in the outermost 8 nm, and the worker stops once that probability exceeds 10⁻⁷. Supported runs finish before appreciable amplitude reaches the walls. The chart is a central crop; region probabilities and the norm always use the entire computational box.

At the beginning, nearly all probability lies on the left. Calling that reflection would be incorrect: it is the incident packet. During collision, incident and reflected amplitudes interfere. After the outgoing packets separate, the left and right spatial integrals approximate reflected and transmitted fractions. The structure integral includes both barriers and the intervening well. The app explicitly shows any residual probability instead of claiming that a finite observation window is an asymptotic measurement.

4. One energy versus a distribution

For a rectangular barrier, the monochromatic transmission at E₀ is not generally the same as the transmitted fraction of a finite Gaussian packet. The latter is compared to the continuum analytic transmission integrated over the initial Gaussian momentum distribution:

Tpacket ≈ ∫₀∞ |φ(k)|² T(Ck²) dk.

The initial distribution is normalized over all momenta. Its tiny negative-momentum tail moves away from the barrier and contributes no right-going transmission. The app shows the monochromatic and spectrum-weighted references separately; neither reference drives the animation.

5. Double-barrier resonance

Repeated reflections in the well interfere. At selected energies they enhance transmission and can delay escape. The resonance preset uses two 0.20 nm barriers of height 1.5 eV around a 0.60 nm well, with E₀ = 0.405 eV and σₓ = 2.4 nm. Its off-resonance comparison keeps that geometry but uses E₀ = 0.650 eV. The transmitted packet fractions are approximately 65.75% and 16.33%. A finite spectral width smooths the near-unit monochromatic peak; narrow resonances may also require a longer observation window.

6. The stationary solver and 3D surface

The other two tabs retain the independent Python stationary solver. It solves −Cψ″ + Vψ = Eψ with finite differences and discrete transparent lead conditions. The left lead carries incident and reflected waves; the right lead has only an outgoing wave. For equal masses and zero asymptotic potential, R and T are ratios of the corresponding probability fluxes.

The stationary phase button rotates ψ(x) by exp(−iφ), leaving |ψ|² unchanged. Actual wave-packet propagation belongs to the first tab. Their parameters and metrics are intentionally independent.

The 3D surface plots T as a function of barrier width and height at one selected energy. Its 41 × 41 numerical samples describe a parameter space, not three-dimensional spatial motion. Rotating the chart changes its viewpoint. Narrow resonances may be missed between grid points; the one-dimensional sweep can explore them further.
